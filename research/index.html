Our study with 25 people

In fall 2026 we compared WebAble with the same pages as the sites made them. It was a small, exploratory study, so read the numbers as a first look.

Who
25 adults aged 50 to 84 who said websites often give them trouble. 9 were 50 to 64, 9 were 65 to 74 and 7 were 75 to 84.
What gave them trouble
Getting around websites (19), finding information (14) and understanding what they read (10). Many named more than one.
Where
On a phone, through a study website, so nothing had to be installed.
Pages
Saved copies of six AP News, Health.com and PEOPLE stories, so everyone saw the same page.
Approval
Exempt determination from the University of Florida’s Human Research Protection Program, October 2026.

What each person did

Each person set WebAble up, then did two rounds of three tasks. In each task they looked for one fact in a news, health or entertainment story. One round showed the pages as the sites made them and the other showed them with WebAble, in an order chosen at random. Each round used different stories, so nobody already knew an answer.

Every question offered “I could not find it” as an answer. People weren’t told which round was WebAble until the end, and WebAble never saw the questions or the answers.

Most of the gain came from fewer people giving up. “I could not find it” fell from 16 answers to 2, and wrong answers fell from 8 to 5. Person by person, 12 found more right answers with WebAble, 10 found as many and 3 found fewer. 21 of the 25 said the two rounds felt different.

For correct answers, the 95% interval for the difference runs from 5.5 to 39.9 points (p = .012). For the ease-of-use rating it runs from 12.6 to 36.7 (p < .001), and for how easy each task felt from 0.83 to 2.37 (p < .001). Time on the page didn’t differ reliably (p = .479). The median times were 31.3 and 18.2 seconds.

What the study doesn’t show

  • It doesn’t show that WebAble makes people faster. Time on the page didn’t change reliably.
  • It can’t say which part helped. WebAble was compared as a whole, so text size, layout and rewording can’t be told apart.
  • It compared WebAble only with the pages as the sites made them, not with reader views or phone settings.
  • It was small: 25 people, six stories and one session each. The p-values aren’t adjusted for making several comparisons.
  • People may rate a version that looks new more highly. That’s why we put the most weight on correct answers.
  • The reworded passages were checked by WebAble’s own checks, not by people outside the project.

The version we studied

The study ran WebAble inside a study website on a phone. Besides changing text size, spacing and clutter, that version could regroup sections and reword some passages in plainer words. A reworded passage that failed WebAble’s checks was thrown out and the original kept. The Chrome extension rewords paragraphs too, in the same careful way, but it runs on a computer and on live sites, so the study tells you how the whole approach did, not how each part of the extension does.

Text size

Evidence: strong

Reading gets faster as print grows, up to a size where it levels off. That size differs a lot between people and rises with age.

What WebAble does. WebAble raises only text smaller than your measured size, and never shrinks text. Text that is already large stays as it is.

What it doesn’t show. Past a certain size, reading slows again, so larger is not always better. That is why WebAble measures instead of choosing one big size for everyone.

Sources: Calabrèse et al., IOVS 2016 (opens in a new tab) · Legge & Bigelow, Journal of Vision 2011 (opens in a new tab) · Systematic review of font size for older readers, PubMed Central (opens in a new tab) · Rello & Baeza-Yates, eye-tracking study with 92 readers (opens in a new tab)

Line and word spacing

Evidence: moderate

More space between lines and words raised reading speed by 26% and 46% for some readers in one study, and made no difference in another. Extra space between letters helped children who find reading hard in one study, but not most readers.

What WebAble does. WebAble offers line spacing from 1.5 to 2.0 on running text, keeps letter spacing as the site set it unless you choose more, and checks every page for overlapping text afterwards.

What it doesn’t show. The benefit varies from person to person, so WebAble learns it from what you keep and undo rather than applying it to everyone.

Sources: Blackmore-Wright, Georgeson & Anderson, PLoS ONE 2013 (opens in a new tab) · Chung, 2002 (opens in a new tab) · Zorzi et al., PNAS 2012 (opens in a new tab) · W3C, Understanding WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.12 (opens in a new tab)

Contrast and light or dark pages

Evidence: strong

Most people read dark text on a light page more accurately, and the advantage grows as text gets smaller. People whose eyes scatter light often read faster with light text on a dark page.

What WebAble does. WebAble darkens faint text to your contrast floor and keeps each colour’s hue. A dark page is only offered when your Tune or your own choice points to it.

What it doesn’t show. Screens are not calibrated, so absolute contrast can’t be measured precisely at home. WebAble uses three wide bands instead of a single threshold.

Sources: Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner, Ergonomics 2013 (opens in a new tab) · Piepenbrock et al., Human Factors 2014 (opens in a new tab) · Legge, Rubin, Pelli & Schleske, Vision Research 1985 (opens in a new tab)

Click targets

Evidence: strong

Small targets cause most missed taps and clicks. In one study, touch errors fell from 42% at 6 mm to 7% at 18 mm for people with less precise pointing.

What WebAble does. WebAble enlarges the clickable area around small links and buttons without moving them, and stops at half the gap to the next control so areas never overlap.

What it doesn’t show. In simulations, a short pointing task places you within one step of the right size about 8 times in 10. WebAble refines it from how you use real pages.

Sources: Kobayashi et al., INTERACT 2011 (opens in a new tab) · Jin, Plocher & Kiff, HCI International 2007 (opens in a new tab) · Findlater et al., CHI 2017 (opens in a new tab)

Steady clicks

Evidence: moderate

More than 1 in 4 clicks by people with less precise pointing included movement during the click. Holding the click on the pressed target helped, and 200 to 400 ms worked best for older adults, while 800 ms made dragging harder.

What WebAble does. Only ever offered, never switched on silently. WebAble uses a window of 200 to 400 ms and never applies it to dragging, sliders or text selection.

What it doesn’t show. Some sites handle clicks in ways that ignore this, so WebAble checks whether it works on each page.

Sources: Trewin, Keates & Moffatt, ASSETS 2006 (opens in a new tab) · Trewin & Pain, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 1999 (opens in a new tab) · Tufail & Kim, Applied Ergonomics 2017 (opens in a new tab)

Moving things

Evidence: moderate

Animated ads and backgrounds slowed reading and search in lab and online studies, and animated banners raised the effort people reported.

What WebAble does. WebAble stops decorative animation and pauses media that starts on its own. You can play anything again.

What it doesn’t show. No randomized trial yet shows that stopping page motion speeds up everyday tasks. A widely quoted review of distraction during reading covers things like TV and messages, not page motion, so WebAble does not rely on it here.

Sources: Simola et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied 2011 (opens in a new tab) · Vital, van der Baan, Stenberg & Sandnes, HCII 2022 (opens in a new tab) · Burke, Hornof, Nilsen & Gorman, TOCHI 2005 (opens in a new tab) · Shen, Frontiers in Psychology 2025 (opens in a new tab)

Busy areas

Evidence: mixed

Cluttered pages slowed search from about 4 to 7 seconds on average, more so for people who prefer simple pages. Older readers are also drawn to eye-catching parts of a page, so hiding the wrong part could do harm.

What WebAble does. WebAble softens ads, recommendations and chat widgets instead of hiding them, and brings them back when you point at them or move into them with the keyboard. Navigation, forms and help are never softened.

What it doesn’t show. Deciding what an area is for can be wrong. When WebAble is unsure, it leaves the area alone.

Sources: Baughan, August, Yamashita & Reinecke, CHI 2020 (opens in a new tab) · Study of age and visual attention, PubMed Central (opens in a new tab)

Line length

Evidence: moderate

Lines of about 55 characters gave the best understanding at typical and fast reading speeds. Lines of 80 or more made readers lose their place.

What WebAble does. WebAble keeps long lines of running text to 62 to 80 characters.

Sources: Dyson & Haselgrove, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 2001 (opens in a new tab) · W3C, Understanding WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.8 (opens in a new tab)

How Tune measures

Tune is short, so each task measures only what it can measure well, and WebAble keeps learning afterwards.

Reading

Evidence: strong

Short true-or-false sentences get smaller until reading slows. The size where it slows is a well-studied measure that repeats within about one size step between sessions.

What WebAble does. Tune uses everyday sentences in a typical web font, because the font changes the size a person needs. It measures rather than asking, since the size people pick for comfort can be off by a factor of 2 or more.

What it doesn’t show. Distance from the screen is unknown, so the result is stored for each kind of device you use.

Sources: Subramanian & Pardhan, 2006 (opens in a new tab) · Latham & Macnaughton, Ophthalmic and Physiological Optics 2022 (opens in a new tab) · Mansfield, Legge & Bane, IOVS 1996 (opens in a new tab) · Yeatman et al., Frontiers in Education 2024 (opens in a new tab)

Pointing

Evidence: moderate

Pointing speed alone predicts poorly for many people. Misses, slips and spread around the target predict what helps.

What WebAble does. Tune measures misses and slips on circles of a few sizes, then places you on the target-size ladder. Keyboard, switch and voice users can skip it and set sizes by hand.

What it doesn’t show. One short session is a starting point. In one study, measures became much more reliable after four sessions, so WebAble keeps refining.

Sources: Gajos, Wobbrock & Weld, UIST 2007 (opens in a new tab) · Pandey et al., TACCESS 2023 (opens in a new tab)

Focus

Evidence: mixed

Short behaviour measures of distraction are noisy for any one person. Which of two pages people prefer tracks how hard a task felt more than how long it took.

What WebAble does. Tune combines a few search tasks on sample pages with side-by-side choices, and weighs your choices heavily. Pages with motion are never shown if your device asks for reduced motion.

What it doesn’t show. Onboarding can only spot strong effects. Your keeps and undos do most of the learning afterwards.

Sources: Hedge, Powell & Sumner, Behavior Research Methods 2018 (opens in a new tab) · Hertzum, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 2025 (opens in a new tab) · Baughan, August, Yamashita & Reinecke, CHI 2020 (opens in a new tab)

How WebAble decides

The idea behind WebAble is that software should fit the person, not the other way round.

Offer, never impose

Evidence: strong

People preferred interfaces they control to ones that change on their own, and inaccurate automatic changes were worse than none.

What WebAble does. Your Tune sets what WebAble does. Anything it notices while you browse only ever becomes a question with two equal answers.

Sources: Findlater & McGrenere, CHI 2004 (opens in a new tab) · Gajos et al., CHI 2008 (opens in a new tab) · Lavie & Meyer, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 2010 (opens in a new tab) · Wobbrock, Gajos, Kane & Vanderheiden, Communications of the ACM 2018 (opens in a new tab)

Checked before it stays

Evidence: moderate

A browser extension that audited, changed and then re-checked pages caught all 57 planted problems and rejected 126 harmful edits.

What WebAble does. After every change, WebAble checks for text that is cut off or overlaps, sideways scrolling, lost contrast, hidden or lost focus and collapsed controls, and puts back any change that fails.

What it doesn’t show. Checks can’t tell whether a page is easier for you. Your keeps and undos answer that.

Sources: Wanscher et al., arXiv 2026 (opens in a new tab)

What WebAble doesn’t offer

Some popular ideas don’t hold up in studies. WebAble leaves them out.

Special reading fonts

Evidence against it: strong

Fonts sold as easier to read gave no gain in speed or accuracy over common fonts, and one study found readers did not prefer them.

What WebAble does. WebAble does not offer them.

Sources: Wery & Diliberto, 2017 (opens in a new tab) · Kuster et al., 2018 (opens in a new tab)

Bold word beginnings

Evidence against it: strong

Across more than 2,000 readers, bolding the start of each word made reading slightly slower.

What WebAble does. WebAble does not offer it.

Sources: Readwise reading-speed study with over 2,000 readers (opens in a new tab)

Coloured tints

Evidence against it: moderate

Coloured overlays showed no reliable reading benefit.

What WebAble does. WebAble does not offer them.

Sources: Henderson et al., Journal of Research in Special Educational Needs 2013 (opens in a new tab)

Trusting preference alone

Evidence against it: moderate

Reading speed differed by 35% between a person’s fastest and slowest font, and the font people said they liked did not predict the fastest one.

What WebAble does. WebAble keeps what it measured and what you chose separately. Your choice always wins, and the measurement sets the starting point.

Sources: Wallace et al., TOCHI 2022 (opens in a new tab)

Focus outline sizes

Evidence: consensus only

No controlled study has measured how the size or contrast of a focus outline changes task time.

What WebAble does. WebAble follows the published guidance of at least 2 px at 3:1, and uses 3 px.

Sources: W3C, Understanding WCAG 2.2 success criterion 2.4.13 (opens in a new tab)
