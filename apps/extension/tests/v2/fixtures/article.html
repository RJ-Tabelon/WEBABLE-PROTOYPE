The quarry road reopens after a summer of volunteer repairs

By Ada North · October 12, 2026

The quarry road, closed since a rockfall in the spring, reopened on Saturday morning after a summer in which more than two hundred volunteers carried gravel, rebuilt drainage channels and replaced every one of the road's forty-one benches. The council had estimated that the repairs would take two years and cost more than the town's entire maintenance budget; instead, the work was finished in a single season.

Organisers kept a careful record of every basket of gravel and every hour worked. By their count, volunteers moved just over nine hundred tonnes of stone by hand and wheelbarrow, most of it in the early mornings before the heat made the slope dangerous. The record, kept in a set of notebooks at the trailhead, is now on display at the library.

A road with a long memory

The road was cut in the eighteen-nineties to carry stone from the quarry to the railway. When the quarry closed, the road became a walking route, and over the following century it gathered the benches, shelters and small bridges that people now associate with it. Several of the benches carry plaques for families who have walked the road for generations.

Restoring those benches was the most delicate part of the work. Each one was taken apart, its timber checked and treated, and its ironwork cleaned by hand. Where a plank could not be saved, volunteers matched the original wood as closely as they could, using oak from a fallen tree in the park.

We wanted people to come back and find their bench exactly where they left it, only stronger.

The drainage work, by contrast, was largely invisible. Engineers who advised the volunteers said that water running under the road surface had caused the rockfall in the first place, and that without new channels the same thing would happen again within a decade. The new channels follow the line of the original Victorian drains, which were rediscovered when the old surface was lifted.

What the numbers say

The volunteers' notebooks make it possible to compare this summer's work with earlier repairs, which were recorded by the council. The table below shows the totals for the three largest projects on the road since records began.

The difference, organisers say, is partly the number of people and partly the notebooks themselves: when everyone could see how much had been done, more people turned up the next morning. Several volunteers said the daily totals, chalked on a board at the trailhead, were the reason they kept coming.

  • More than 200 volunteers took part.
  • Every one of the 41 benches was restored.
  • The road reopened eight weeks ahead of the revised schedule.

What comes next

The council has agreed to fund the upkeep of the new drainage channels, and the volunteers plan to meet once a month to clear leaves and check the benches. A smaller group has already started on the footbridge at the far end of the road, which has been closed to walkers for three years and needs new handrails.

For now, the road is busy again. On Saturday, the first walkers arrived before the ribbon was cut, and by the afternoon every bench had someone sitting on it. The notebooks, open on a table at the library, already have a new page: a list of names of people who would like to help with the bridge.
